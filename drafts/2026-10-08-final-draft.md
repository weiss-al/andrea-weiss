Intro and Literature Review – Pay What you Want 
The Pay-What-You-Want (PWYW) model of pricing allows consumers to determine the price they are willing to pay for a good, including the seller accepting $0. While initially counterintuitive to pay anything more than what is required to receive the good, Gneezy describes how the social, psychological, and moral transaction costs can make it “rational” for buyers to pay greater than $0 under PWYW circumstances [Gneezy]. Further, “PWYW has been institutionalized in nonprofit contexts as part of ongoing mission-related practice” [Sandberg]. 
This research aims to assess the viability of implementing a PWYW model at a local Hawaii museum for their audio tours as a way to increase the percentage of local, vice tourist, visitation of the museum and further the non-profit’s educational mission for a larger population. Gerpott’s review of PWYW research identified six factors in that can increase the likelihood of success for a PWYW model. [Gerpott] The current use case is evaluated for how it measures against Gerpott’s factors in Table 1.

Method and assumptions
A limited-scale model was developed for a museum offering audio tours in a PWYW model for local consumers (identified by their State ID). The model performed sensitivity analysis for average PWYW contribution and average profit from gift shop purchases of conventionally priced items. Assumptions for the model include the total visitor count, number of local visitors at the start of a marketing campaign, effectiveness of a marketing campaign for a given dollar spend, and that tourist traffic will remain unchanged by the campaign.
Hypothesis
Increased awareness of tour availability and overall image of the museum explicitly acknowledging local consumers will draw additional sales and prompt more purchases of conventionally priced items at the gift shop. This increase will at least be able to break-even with the marketing campaign required to bring in additional local visitors. 
Model Results
Figures 1 and 2 display the sensitivity analyses for additional gift shop spending and PWYW average contribution, respectively, against the marketing campaign costs and percentage rise in local visitors. These show that even with a small increase in local visitors or a small spend in the gift shop, the model becomes viable with a marketing spend of $5000. This is driven by the low cost of the audio tours, where the direct cost is limited to the $1 for headphones. 
Critiques of Model
This model does not distinguish for Buyer Anonymity, which can affect the social pressures to pay closer to the recommended price and decrease the average amount paid [Bondos and Lipowski, 2016]. The assumption is that all purchases will be made at the site vice online, forcing the in-person interaction where a buyer has to decide in the moment what they will pay. The model would need to be reassessed if local visitors could purchase tours via PWYW online where the social pressure is lower, though there is mixed evidence contending that anonymity does not have a significant effect [Gneezy et al., 2012].
The model is also run for a short term of 90 days only, which limits its assessment given that the target PWYW customers are a finite group that are unlikely to be regularly repeating customers. The local community is small and relatively stagnant, further, once someone has done the audio tour, they likely will not opt to do it again. Therefore, long-term viability of this model would have to be reassessed once more concrete assumptions could be made based on its initial success.
Finally, this model does not fully incorporate indirect costs of staffing time to train on and implement the model. It also does not model any potential lost revenue from long lines that cause visitors to not purchase a tour. 
Additional Factors to Consider in Future Modeling and Research
The existing studies of PWYW pricing assess a variety of factors across industries, including the buyers, the sellers, the objects for sale, the procedure of the sale interaction, and the overall market [Stegemann, 2014, p. 19]. Many aspects of each of these categories are not thoroughly modeled in this assessment and could be assessed individually to further refine the sensitivity of the analysis. 
Conclusion
Given the very low marginal cost of additional audio tours, this is an ideal model to attempt a PWYW structure. Additional factors noted in the research review should be considered in the marketing and operations implementation to maximize the average PWYW contribution. The major cost is a marketing campaign to inform the local community of the opportunity, which could be achieved through donations or scaled down if the board had a lower risk tolerance for the innovation. The goal of the campaign assessed in this research is to increase visitor traffic to achieve the non-profit’s mission of education and remembrance. A break-even point suffices to achieve that goal, however additional donations and revenue from the bookstore provide an ROI that justifies the time and energy required to implement the campaign.
