Pay-what-you-want is a genuinely interesting puzzle for an economics paper, because it removes the one thing a market normally relies on, a posted price, and asks what is left holding the business up. Your hypothesis already reaches for the right tools: marginal cost, how quickly the next unit stops being worth having ("I don't need 12 cups of coffee, even if it is free"), and the social pressure of a small community. The spec is still the template, so the brief is carrying the whole design for now, and the items below are about sharpening it.

**Narrow the topic to one decision and one question.** The brief sets out to evaluate "the various factors" — product type, location, community, mission — which is several papers. Who would act on your answer, and what are they deciding? Then, what does "successful" mean in a way you can measure? Paying for what, at what level, and against which costs? That definition is the backbone of the model you describe, and the same question belongs in the spec's Purpose section, so the two documents ask the same thing.

**Write a falsification condition that can fail.** "If the PWYW model held for a broader range of goods/services or in larger communities, it would indicate there are additional factors to consider" ends by keeping the hypothesis alive rather than rejecting it. Once success is defined, what result would sink each claim? For the community claim: what would the studies have to show about payments in large cities versus small towns for that part of the hypothesis to fail? And on the concepts: what does price elasticity measure when the buyer sets the price? Ask which concepts actually do the explaining, and keep only those.

**Name the studies and plan one figure.** "Existing literature" needs names before you start reading. Two well-known places to begin are Kim, Natter and Spann (2009) in the *Journal of Marketing*, on pay-what-you-want as a participative pricing mechanism, and the Gneezy, Gneezy, Nelson and Brown (2010) field experiment in *Science* on pay-what-you-want with a charitable share. Real businesses that tried it and closed would give you the unsuccessful cases your method expects. Listing these in the spec's Inputs table, and naming one figure (for example, average payment against marginal cost across the cases you collect), gives the paper its evidence before the drafting begins.

**In order:**

1. Choose one decision-maker, one question and a measurable definition of success, and put the same question in the spec.
2. Answer in the brief what result would reject each claim, and decide which concepts the hypothesis actually uses.
3. Name the primary studies in the spec's Inputs table and plan one figure.
